TriChess — Welcome to the Hex

TriChess is chess, but the board bends, the pieces multiply, and three minds clash instead of two. Played on a hexagonal board with 169 cells, this is a reimagining of chess for the triangular age. Every piece has more power, every move has more options, and every game has an extra player to negotiate with — or against.

What Makes TriChess Different?

1. The Hexagonal Arena

Standard chess is 64 squares in a flat grid. TriChess is 169 hexagons arranged in a honeycomb. Instead of 4 orthogonal directions, pieces move in 6 axial directions — more space, more routes, more tactics.

2. More Moves, More Pieces

Every piece gets a boost on the hex board:

Piece Standard Chess TriChess
Pawn attacks 2 diagonals 3 forward-diagonals
Knight moves 8 L-shapes 12 hex-L-shapes
Bishop directions 4 6 (you get 3 bishops!)
Rook directions 4 6
Queen directions 8 12
King moves 8 12 + castling
Starting pieces 16 17 per player

Each player starts with 17 pieces — that's 51 pieces on the board at the start. The extra bishop means bishops successfully cover every diagonal on the hex grid.

3. Three-Player Diplomacy

With three players, the game becomes social as well as strategic:

  • Resignation voting: Players can vote to resign. But here's the twist — if just one player refuses to resign (votes "Decline"), they win instead. You can't force someone out unless they agree to go.
  • Draw voting: All three must accept for a draw. One holdout keeps the game going.
  • Proportional checkmate: When multiple players attack a king, the checkmate points are split proportionally by how many pieces each attacker contributed.
  • Automatic endings: Stalemate (no legal move, but not in check) and threefold repetition end the game on their own — no vote needed.

4. Unique Scoring

  • Win (checkmate): 2.0 points (split among attackers if multiple)
  • Draw / Stalemate / Threefold repetition: 2/3 point each
  • Resignation: The non-resigning player gets 2.0
  • Rating: Multi-player Elo starting at 500

5. No Third Player? Bring a Bot

Can't find a 3rd human, or just want to practice? The game owner can seat one or two computer-controlled players (Bot 1 / Bot 2) into any empty seat and play solo or mixed human/bot games — see the Playing with Bots tab for details.

6. Two Ways to Play

Choose your board renderer in your profile:

  • ondro — Built with Konva.js. Richer visuals, smooth animations, pinch-to-zoom, panning, scroll-wheel support on mobile, and the board updates live the instant another player or a bot moves.
  • filio — Raw Canvas 2D. Lighter weight, snappy, but landscape; it also updates live, redrawing the whole board when another player or a bot moves.

Both give you the same game — pick the one that feels right.

7. Make It Yours

  • 6 themes: Default, Night, Sandy, Contrast, Colorblind, Black & White
  • 3 piece sets: Default, Chubby, School
  • Switch anytime in your profile settings

8. Colorblind-Friendly

The colorblind theme uses patterns and high-contrast colors so everyone can tell territories and pieces apart.

Ready to Play?

  1. Register an account
  2. Head to Available to join an open game, create your own, or seat a bot into an empty seat if you own the game
  3. Choose your seat — Seat 1, 2, or 3
  4. Once all seats are filled (by humans and/or bots), the game starts automatically and appears on the Home page
  5. Click a piece, click a valid move, then hit Submit

The hexagonal revolution is waiting. See you on the board.

Making a Move

This game uses click-to-select, click-to-move interaction:

  1. Click a piece (one of your pieces, when it's your turn)
    • The hex highlights with a selection border
    • Coordinate axis hints appear through the hex — shows the q, r, s axes
    • Valid destinations light up:
      • Green circle = safe move (empty square)
      • Red X = attack/capture
  2. Click a highlighted hex to move there
    • If you click the same piece again, you deselect it
    • If you click another one of your pieces, it switches selection
  3. If promoting (pawn reaches the opposite side), a popup appears:
    • Choose Q (Queen), R (Rook), B (Bishop), or N (Knight)
    • Click your choice — the move is completed

Submitting Your Move

Your move is local until you submit it. The game does not auto-submit:

  • filio: Click the OK button below the canvas
  • ondro: Click Submit in the right sidebar

After submission, the server validates your move and applies it. If another player submitted before you (race condition), you'll get a conflict warning — just reload and try again.

Live Board Updates

Your board refreshes automatically the moment another player — or a bot — makes a move or casts a vote, so you don't need to keep reloading the page to see what happened. ondro updates the position in place; filio redraws the whole board.

Voting

TriChess has two voting mechanisms. Voting blocks normal moves — you must resolve the vote before playing.

Draw Vote

  1. Click the Draw button (only on your turn, at the latest move)
  2. A modal shows all three players with Accept/Decline buttons
  3. As other players take their turns, they'll see the vote prompt
  4. Result: All 3 accept → draw (2/3 point each). Anyone declines → vote fails, game continues

Resignation Vote

  1. Click the Resign button (same conditions as Draw)
  2. Each player votes Accept or Decline
  3. Result: 2 accept, 1 declines → the declining player wins (2.0 points). Otherwise → vote fails

Strategic note: Resignation voting creates a prisoner's dilemma. If two players gang up on the third and vote to resign them, the third can simply decline and take the win. True resignation only happens when someone genuinely wants out.

Playing with Bots

Don't have two other players handy? Want to practice a new opening or just try out the hex board on your own terms? Any game owner can seat a computer-controlled opponent into an empty seat.

Seating a Bot

  1. Create a game from the Available page (you become its owner)
  2. Next to each empty seat in your own game's row, click Bot instead of waiting for Join
  3. Each bot seat gets a distinct account — Bot 1 and Bot 2 — so a game can have up to two bot-controlled seats without any ambiguity about whose move is whose
  4. Once all 3 seats are filled (by any mix of humans and bots), the game starts automatically

Only the game's owner can seat a bot — this keeps someone else's open game from being filled with a bot against their wishes.

How Bots Play

  • Bots follow exactly the same rules as human players — same legal moves, same checkmate/stalemate conditions, and they can be checkmated just like anyone else.
  • Bots can also cast draw and resignation votes when it's their turn to respond.
  • Early in the game, bots often reply almost instantly using a book of common opening moves. Later on, a bot calculates its move by searching ahead — this can take up to roughly 30 seconds on tougher middlegame positions, so don't worry if a bot doesn't respond immediately.
  • A bot's move is computed in the background — you don't need to do anything to trigger it, and (on the ondro renderer) your board updates live the moment it responds.

Casual, Not Competitive

Any game with at least one bot seat is casual:

  • No effect on any player's rating
  • Not counted in the leaderboard on the Ratings page
  • Not counted toward your win/loss/score statistics on your Profile page

Bot games are purely for fun and practice — play as many as you like without any impact on your standing.

If you play alone against both bots in a game you own, you can delete it at any time: click the red × in place of its number on the Games page and confirm.

Notifications in Bot Games

If you're the only human in a game (facing both Bot 1 and Bot 2), you generally won't receive live "it's your turn" or "game over" push notifications — you're presumably already at the board, so these are switched off by default for solo games. If you do walk away and forget about the game, you'll still get a reminder notification after about 24 hours of inactivity, just like any other game.

If your game has two humans and one bot, notifications work exactly as normal — the bot seat doesn't change anything for the human players.

Why Play a Bot?

  • Learn the hexagonal board and piece movement without waiting for other players
  • Try out an opening or tactic before using it in a rated game
  • Fill an empty seat when a 3rd human isn't available so the other two can still play

Trichess rules

Trichess is a three-player variation of chess played on a hexagonal board. The board has three colors, which define the diagonals (blue lines), while straight lines (yellow lines) run across the flat sides of the hexagons. Because of the three-colored design, each player begins with three bishops instead of two. These rules apply identically whether you're facing human opponents, a bot, or a mix of both.

Play

At the start of the game, Player 1 always makes the first move, and turns rotate clockwise among the three players. On each turn, a player may move only one piece, with the sole exception of castling, which involves moving both the king and rook together.

Capturing follows the standard principle: a player removes an opponent's piece by moving onto its square. A player may not move through or capture their own pieces. Pawns are the only exception to the general rule of moving and capturing in the same way, as pawns move forward but capture diagonally forward.

Turns

In Trichess, all pieces move as in traditional chess, adapted to the hexagonal board's straight and diagonal directions.

Pawns move one space forward at a time, but on their very first move they may advance two spaces. Captures are made diagonally forward (see A symbols on figure). If a pawn reaches the one of the opponent side of the board, it is promoted to a stronger piece of the player's choice, except for the king. Any number of pawns can be promoted during the game.

Note: The en passant capture, which normally allows a pawn to capture an enemy pawn that has advanced two spaces, is not yet implemented in Trichess.

Rooks move any number of spaces in straight lines, whether forward, backward, or sideways.

Knights are unique in that they jump over intervening pieces. A knight's movement follows an L-shape: two spaces in one direction, either forward, backward, or sideways, followed by a one-space turn left or right.

Bishops move any number of spaces along diagonals.

Queens combine the powers of rook and bishop, moving any number of spaces in straight or diagonal directions.

The king moves only one space at a time in any direction, but may never move into a square that is under attack.

Castling is the only move in which two pieces may move simultaneously. To castle, the king moves two spaces toward a rook, and the rook jumps over the king to land on the other side. Castling is permitted only if the king has not previously moved, the rook has not moved, no pieces stand between them, and the king is not in check, does not pass through a checked square, and does not land in check. It does not matter if the king was in check earlier in the game, or if the rook itself is under attack before castling.

Check

A king is considered in check when an opposing piece threatens to capture it on the opponent's next turn. When in check, a player must respond by moving the king out of check, capturing the attacking piece, or placing another piece in the way to block the attack. A king may never move into a position where it would be in check.

Checkmate

Putting an opponent's king in checkmate is the only way to win the game.

Checkmate occurs when a king is in check and cannot escape. In this case, the attacking players earn two points total, split according to how many of their pieces contribute to the checkmate. For example, if one player delivers the checkmate, they earn two points, while if two players' pieces both contribute to the checkmate, each receives one point.

In the illustration, the blue queen and yellow knight have the green king in check, and all the spaces where the king can move can be attacked by the queen or knight. The king cannot take the queen, because the other queen is protecting the queen. This is checkmate and 2 points are shared between blue and yellow players according to the number of their pieces giving check. So 1 point for blue (blue queen) and 1 point for yellow (yellow knight).

If a player had another piece somewhere on the board that is not blocked, it would have to move. The game would continue.

Stalemate, Draws, and Repetition

Not every game ends in checkmate. Trichess recognizes four other ways a game can end, all sharing the same 2/3-point-each split (except resignation):

  • Stalemate: The player on move has no legal move anywhere on the board, but their king is not in check. Unlike checkmate, this ends the game immediately and automatically — 2/3 point for each of the three players.
  • Threefold repetition: If the exact same position — same pieces on the same squares, same player to move, and the same castling/pawn-double-step rights — occurs three times over the course of the game, the game ends automatically in a draw, 2/3 point each. No one needs to claim it; the server detects it as soon as the third occurrence happens.
  • Draw vote: All three players accept a draw — 2/3 point each. See the How to Play tab for how voting works.
  • Resignation vote: Two players accept resignation and one declines — the declining player wins outright (2.0 points). See the How to Play tab.

Stalemate and threefold repetition require no action from any player — the server checks for both after every move and ends the game the instant either condition is met.

Basic Strategy

Trichess is an incredibly complex strategic game, and it is impossible to go into all the possible tactics one could use to win. Here are some hints for new players.

As in traditional chess, pieces in Trichess have relative values that help guide strategy. Queens are worth nine points, rooks five, bishops and knights three each, and pawns one. However, pawns become more valuable as they approach promotion. Because pawn promotion is such a powerful opportunity, it is almost always best to promote a pawn into a queen.

Effective play in Trichess requires both strong offense and solid defense. Players should try to maintain balance across the board, keeping their forces spread out to control key areas. Attacks are most successful when supported by multiple pieces, as lone pieces are easily cut off and captured. At the same time, players must guard their king carefully while also preparing to exploit weaknesses in the defenses of their two opponents.

The ultimate objective of Trichess is to checkmate an opponent's king. Once checkmate is achieved, the winning players are awarded points according to pieces involved in checkmate.

Enjoy the game.

Game Engine Architecture

Stateless Design

GameAPI is completely stateless. Every request creates a fresh instance:

ga = GameAPI(view_pid)
    ga.replay_from_slog(slog)

The slog (serialized game log) is the single source of truth — a flat string where every 4 characters encode one action.

Hex Geometry

Cells use axial coordinates (q, r) with the implied third axis s = -q - r. A cell is on the board when -7 ≤ q, r, s ≤ 7: a hexagon with 7 cells per edge, 169 cells in all. In seat 0's view a cell is drawn at x = q + r/2, y = r·√3/2 (y grows downwards), so rows of equal r run horizontally. The colour of a cell is (2q + r) mod 3.

                    base12  (r = -7, neutral)
              (0,-7) ______________ (7,-7)
                    /              \
  base1 (s = 7)    /                \    base2 (q = 7)
  seat 1 home     /                  \   seat 2 home
          (-7,0) <                    > (7,0)
  base01 (q = -7) \                  /   base20 (s = -7)
  neutral          \                /    neutral
                    \______________/
              (-7,7)                (0,7)
                     base0  (r = 7)
                     seat 0 home

Each seat owns one edge (its back rank) and the edges between the homes are neutral. Seat 0 sits at the bottom, seat 1 upper left and seat 2 upper right; every client rotates the board so that its own seat is at the bottom.

Directions

Moves are written in step codes, relative to the moving player; STEP in engine/player.py turns them into (dq, dr) per seat. FL/FR (forward left/right) and SL (sideways left) are the edge neighbours; DF, DL, DR (diagonal forward/left/right) go through a corner of the cell to the next cell beyond it. The suffix r is the reverse direction (FLr = back right). For seat 0, as drawn on screen:

                DF
          DL  FL  FR  DR
            SL  ●   SLr
          DRr FRr FLr DLr
                DFr

Seat 1's table is seat 0's turned by 120° ((dq, dr) → (ds, dq)), and seat 2's is seat 1's turned again, so "forward" always points from the seat's home towards the far side.

  • Rook slides along the 6 edge directions, Bishop along the 6 corner directions, Queen along all 12, King one step in any of the 12.
  • Knight: two steps in one edge direction, then one in a neighbouring edge direction (e.g. SL SL FRr): 12 targets, jumping.
  • Pawn: moves FL or FR onto an empty cell, both cells of FL FL/FR FR empty on its first move; captures DL, DF or DR. _PAWN_ATTACK in engine/board.py is the reverse view: the offsets from which a pawn of each seat attacks a cell.
  • Move kinds in Move.kind: s step, f forward onto empty only, a attack (capture) only, d pawn double step, n slide until the edge or the first piece, c castling.
Promotion and castling

A pawn promotes on any cell of the three far edges: for seat 0 base1 + base12 + base2 (Board.opposite). Castling moves the king two cells along SL (short side, rook next to the landing cell) or SLr (long side, one more empty cell before the rook); the king must not be in check, have moved, or pass through an attacked cell, and the rook must not have moved. CASTLING_ROOK_MOVES lists the 6 possible king moves with the rook move they imply; for seat 0: king (-4,7)→(-6,7) with rook (-7,7)→(-5,7), and king (-4,7)→(-2,7) with rook (0,7)→(-3,7).

gid order

The frontend never sees coordinates: it talks in gids, the 169 cells numbered as the viewer sees them, row by row from the top left (gid 0) to the bottom right (gid 168). Each view_pid maps them to different board cells (GameAPI.update_ui_mappings, _GID_TRAVERSAL), so the same gid names a different cell for each seat:

view_pid Traversal (outer, inner) gid 0 gid 168
0r up, q up(0,-7)(0,7)
1s up, r up(7,0)(-7,0)
2q up, r down(-7,7)(7,-7)

Slog Format

The slog is the whole game as one string of 4-character chunks: moves and votes, in order. It is the only stored game state; everything else is derived by replaying it (replay_from_slog, or slog_summary for list pages). Code splits it with iter_chunks() (a trailing partial chunk is ignored) and tells votes from moves with is_vote_chunk(); the prefixes are defined once, in engine/gameapi.py.

Move: chr(72+q1) chr(72+r1) chr(72+q2) chr(72+r2) — from cell, to cell. Coordinates −7…7 become A…O (H = 0). Promotion adds 32 to one of the four characters, making it lowercase: q1 → Queen, r1 → Rook, q2 → Bishop, r2 → Knight. This is unambiguous because a coordinate character never exceeds O, so any character above it is a flag (slog2pos tests > "O"), and the vote prefixes R S r s lie outside both A–O and a–o.

Votes: r (resign) or s (draw) followed by one character per seat, A accept, D decline, X not this voter. The player on move casts one; while a vote runs, on_move = (moves + votes cast) mod 3 and no piece can move. Once all 3 have voted, the three single votes are condensed into one set-vote chunk, R/S with all three answers. A draw needs 3 accepts; a resignation needs exactly 2, and the player who declined wins.

Worked example, a draw offered after the first move:

FNHL          seat 0: pawn (-2,6) -> (0,4), F=-2 N=6 H=0 L=4
FNHLsXAX      seat 1 offers a draw
FNHLsXAXsXXA  seat 2 accepts
FNHLSAAA      seat 0 accepts: condensed into one set-vote, the game is drawn
kBKA          a promotion: q1 'k' = 'K'+32, so (3,-6) -> (3,-7) promoting to a Queen

Endgame Detection

GameAPI.endgame() is the single classifier all game-ending logic dispatches through (API response, DB persistence, and scoring) — it returns one of "checkmate", "draw", "resignation", "stalemate", "repetition", or None while the game is still ongoing. Draw/resignation come from Voting; checkmate/stalemate both reduce to "the on-move player has no legal move anywhere", distinguished only by whether their king is currently attacked.

Threefold repetition is detected via GameAPI._position_signature() — a hashable tuple of every occupied hex's (position, piece label, owning player) plus the used flag of any Pawn/King/Rook (the only pieces whose future legal moves depend on it: pawn double-step and king/rook castling eligibility). replay_from_slog() tallies one signature's occurrence count per move as it replays; repetition() is true once the current position's count reaches 3. The tally is rebuilt from scratch on every request (consistent with the engine's stateless, replay-the-whole-slog design) and is intentionally not threaded through make_move() or copy(), since the bot's minimax search only ever evaluates hypothetical positions, never the real game.

Bot Player System

engine/bot.py picks a move/vote via an opening book (engine/opening.py, built from finished games) for early plies, falling back to minimax search (default depth 3 — BOT_DEPTH — the minimum needed to see both other players' replies before scoring a position) for everything else.

webapp/botplayer.py wires this into the webapp:

  • maybe_trigger_bot(board_id) — cheap check, called right after every GameBoard.post() commit and after a game starts; enqueues run_bot_move on the bot RQ queue only if the seat now on move is actually a bot.
  • run_bot_move(board_id) — the RQ job body: computes the move/vote, then persists it by POSTing to the app's own /api/v1/manager/board endpoint in-process (via the Flask test client, with a freshly minted JWT) — reuses 100% of the same scoring/notification/rating logic a human's request goes through, no duplicated code path.
  • bot-sweep CLI command (cron, every 5 min) — safety net that re-triggers any active game whose on-move seat is a bot, in case a queued job was lost (e.g. a Redis restart).

Games with any bot seat skip Score row creation and rating updates (board_has_bot guard in GameBoard.post()). Live push notifications are further gated by SINGLE_PLAYER_NOTIFICATIONS (default false) specifically for the 1-human-vs-2-bots case; resend_notification()'s 24h-stale-game reminder always ignores that flag and never targets a bot account. remove-bot-games CLI command (cron, every 5 min; BOT_GAMES_REMOVAL minutes, default 15, 0 disables) deletes old finished bot games and their logs.

Search strength: BOT_DEPTH (default 3, never below 3) always completes; BOT_TIME_LIMIT seconds (default 2.0, 0 = fixed depth) let iterative deepening go further, up to BOT_MAX_DEPTH (default 12). Benchmark: uv run python -m scripts.bench_bot.

How the Bot Chooses a Move

engine/bot.py's choose_move runs in this order:

  1. A pending vote is answered by choose_vote instead: it tries accept and decline and follows every remaining voter's best answer to the end of the vote, comparing an ended game (scored like GameAPI.outcome_scores) with the evaluation of the position play would resume from.
  2. Opening book (engine/opening.py): for the first 9 plies, the move most often played at that ply in finished games (pooled per ply, not per move sequence), if at least 3 games played it and it is legal here. Skipped whenever one of the bot's own non-pawn pieces is attacked, as the book only knows what was popular, not what is safe.
  3. Search (engine/search.py): paranoid alpha-beta search — the bot maximises its own score and every opponent move is assumed to minimise it, both opponents pooled into one minimising side. At least 3 plies (MIN_SOUND_DEPTH): one full round of the table, or the second opponent's replies are never seen and a piece hung to them looks safe.

Iterative deepening: depths up to BOT_DEPTH always complete; deeper ones, up to BOT_MAX_DEPTH, only while BOT_TIME_LIMIT lasts. An iteration cut off by the deadline is discarded, one that can't finish in time is not started, and a forced win or loss stops the deepening. Each extra ply costs roughly ×10 in a developed middlegame, so the default 2 s mostly ends at depth 3 there and reaches 4–5 in simpler positions.

Move ordering decides how much alpha-beta can prune: promotions first (all four pieces are tried), then captures by MVV-LVA (most valuable victim, least valuable attacker), then quiet moves by the history heuristic (a quiet move that caused a cutoff earns depth², shared across the whole search and its iterations). Only about 5% of moves are captures on this board, so the history heuristic orders most of the tree. There is no transposition table and no quiescence search.

Evaluation (engine/eval.py, ported exactly into search.py), one weighted sum per position:

  • Material ×1.0: own piece values minus half of both opponents' together (P 1, N 3, B 3, R 5, Q 9).
  • Mobility ×0.1: the number of moves of the bot's own pieces.
  • King safety ×0.3: (own pieces within 2 cells of the king, minus 1 when in check) × 2.
  • Activity ×0.05: a bonus near the centre (3/2/1/1 at distance 0–3) and, for pawns, a bonus growing as they near promotion (0, 0, 0, 1, 2, 3, 5, 8 by distance).

Finished positions are scored with the server's own points (GameAPI.outcome_scores), so the bot values results the way the scoring does. The search runs on its own compact position (169 ints, make/unmake in place, move tables built at import from the engine's definitions), about 20× faster than searching over GameAPI copies; tests/test_search.py checks that it agrees with the object engine move for move. Without a time budget the bot is deterministic: the same position and depth always give the same move. Both bot accounts play identically.

Live Updates (SSE)

webapp/events.py publishes a Redis pub/sub ping (one channel per board id) whenever GameBoard.post() commits — from a real human POST or the bot's in-process re-entry into the same endpoint. GET /api/v1/manager/board/events?id=<id>&jwt=<token> (served by sse/app.py, a separate Starlette/uvicorn service the reverse proxy routes this one path to) streams these as Server-Sent Events to any seated player's open board page; auth is query-string-only on this one route since the browser's EventSource API can't set custom headers.

Both renderers open one EventSource per board on page load and refetch the full state on each ping, never trusting the pushed payload: ondro.js calls boardReset() (skipping pings at or below its synced slog length, and resyncing whenever the stream reconnects), filio.js calls Click_Refresh(). Each open tab is a coroutine in the async service, sharing one Redis pattern subscription per process, so open boards never tie up gunicorn threads — 1000 concurrent streams measured at about 35 MB extra RSS. After a lost Redis subscription is restored the service closes every stream so browsers reconnect and resync.

Local dev: run uv run uvicorn sse.app:app --port 8090 --no-access-log next to flask run, and set SSE_URL=http://localhost:8090 and the Flask origin in CORS_ORIGINS in .env.

Race Condition Prevention

POST /manager/board uses SELECT ... FOR UPDATE row locking. It compares the submitted slog against the server slog — exactly 1 new move must be appended, or a vote must be in progress. Conflicts return HTTP 409.

Multi-Player Elo

e_i = 10^(rating_i / L) / sum(10^(rating_j / L) for j in 0..2)
    rating_i += K * (actual_score_i - e_i)

K=9.0, L=400.0, starting rating 500 (webapp/ratings.py). Each finished rated game is applied incrementally under row locks on its three players (record_game_rating()) and appended to RatingHistory. flask rebuild-ratings replays every rated game from scratch — the repair path, also run when the admin deletes a rated game.

Frontend Architecture

Two Renderers

The user.board preference selects the template and JS:

  • board == "ondro" → board_ondro.html + ondro.js (Konva.js with scene graph, animations, touch support, live SSE updates)
  • board == "filio" → board_filio.html + filio.js (raw Canvas 2D, simpler; live updates refresh the whole board)

Both consume the same API and support the same interactions.

Theme & Piece System

YAML files in static/themes/ (6 themes) and static/pieces/ (3 sets). Loaded by views.py and injected as JSON into the template. Themes define hex colors, piece colors, highlight colors, fonts. Piece sets contain SVG path data for each piece type (P, N, B, R, Q, K).

JWT Flow

  1. /play/<id> route creates a short-lived access token (1h)
  2. Injected as Authorization: Bearer <token> into the page (and, stripped of the Bearer prefix, as a query-string token for the SSE connection)
  3. All other JS API calls use the header
  4. On 401, the page alerts "Token expired" and reloads

Security notes

  • Use long random secret keys in production, and DEBUG=false.
  • Restrict CORS_ORIGINS to trusted domains.
  • Keep API_TOKEN_USERS to trusted accounts — it gates direct API access, not browser play (every logged-in player gets their own token server-side for the board).
  • Set TRUSTED_PROXY_HOPS only when a proxy really sits in front, or clients can spoof their address.
  • Keep the SSE route out of Traefik's access log (router label observability.accessLogs=false on its router): its URL carries the user's JWT.

Background Jobs

  • Redis (REDIS_URL) and one worker: rq worker notifications bot — two queues in priority order, so a slow bot search never delays a notification (see trichess-worker in docker-compose.yml).
  • webapp/notifications.py: ntfy.sh push notifications (post_notification) and SMTP email (send_email) are queued instead of blocking the request. The wrappers keep their synchronous "is this configured" checks; send failures are logged in the worker.
  • Push notifications go to ntfy.mykuna.eu, never in debug mode.

Admin & Maintenance

  • Admin is a hardcoded check (g.user.id == 1) in views.py, not a role column. The admin's profile page has a user selector for any player's rating chart and the maintenance-mode toggle.
  • New users start with active=False until they verify their email or the admin approves them.
  • Removing a user deletes an account with no games, otherwise anonymises it ([deleted <id>], no email, unusable password, inactive) so games and ratings stay consistent. Deleting a game also deletes its scores, logs and rating history, and rebuilds ratings if it was rated.
  • Maintenance mode (setting table, so every process sees it): every request but the admin's gets a 503 "Maintenance in progress" page with Retry-After (JSON 503 under /api/), and open board pages reload into it. Only login, logout and static files stay reachable, and only the admin can sign in. Bots, resend-notifications and bot-sweep pause; once it's off, the next bot-sweep resumes waiting bot games.

Logging System

  • Custom level GAME (25), between INFO and WARNING
  • DBHandler (logging.Handler) writes all GAME+ level records to the log table
  • Attached to root logger — catches all child loggers (views, api, email)
  • Writes on its own connection and transaction, so a log line survives the request's rollback. Row locks taken in a request must therefore be FOR NO KEY UPDATE (with_for_update(key_share=True)), or a log row referencing the locked row deadlocks on PostgreSQL.
  • GAME records game creation, moves and completion with user_id/board_id
  • Cleanup: flask --app=webapp purge-logs --days 90

Privacy & Cookies

What we store to run the game

Your account (username, email, hashed password, preferences), your games and ratings, and a server log of game events. A session cookie keeps you logged in. These are required for Trichess to work.

Analytics (optional)

With your consent, we use Google Analytics to see how visitors find and use the site — which pages are visited, account/lobby actions such as signing up, logging in, or creating and joining games, and game milestones such as a game starting, your first move, a draw or resign vote, and how a finished game ended (its length and your placing). It is never loaded before you click Accept.

  • No usernames or email addresses are sent; logged-in visitors are identified only by an anonymous, one-way hashed id.
  • Individual moves and board positions are not sent to Google. Your rating and number of games are sent only as broad bands (e.g. "Intermediate", "6-20 games").
  • Advertising features and Google signals are turned off.
  • Password-reset and verification links are masked before anything is sent.

You can change your choice at any time via Cookie settings in the page footer. Rejecting after accepting removes the Google Analytics cookies from your browser.
